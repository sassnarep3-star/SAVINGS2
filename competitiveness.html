Rate competitiveness

Verified APYs measured against the FDIC national average and the Treasury curve, across 1-, 3- and 5-year horizons.

3. What the comparison actually says

  • The best savings rate does not beat the Treasury curve. 3.75% sits 0.79 points below the 12-month Treasury yield (4.54%), and the gap widens with maturity: over five years the Treasury curve would earn about $19,915.23 more on $250,000 than the savings account at today's rates. Treasuries are not FDIC-insured and carry price risk if sold before maturity — that is what you are paid for.
  • Versus the average bank, the savings account wins by a mile. 3.75% is 9.9× the FDIC national average of 0.38%. The gap between a 0.01% branch account and a 3.75% online account is worth far more than the gap between the best savings account and a Treasury.
  • The curve slopes upward. The 2-year (4.88%), 3-year (5.00%) and 5-year (5.09%) Treasury yields all sit above the 1-year (4.54%), so committing money for longer is rewarded. A savings account gives you the same short-end exposure with no lock-up and no price risk, but no fixed rate either.
  • Lifespan changes the ranking, not the winner. Across 1, 3 and 5 years the highest verified savings account stays the best savings product, but at today's rates it never overtakes the Treasury curve. That is the price of liquidity.
  • Rate history is the missing dimension. None of the banks publish a public rate history in a form that can be verified here, so this study cannot tell you how often these APYs moved. What it can tell you is that every bank on the list reserves the right to change the rate the day after you open the account.

4. Competitive position within this study

Of 19 products screened, 10 publish a rate that could be verified. The distribution is bimodal: eight verified accounts cluster between 3.10% and 3.75%; two verified branch accounts pay 0.01%; the rest publish nothing a public page can capture. There is almost nothing in between — which is why choosing the category of bank matters more than fine-tuning between banks inside a category.

What would change this conclusion. If the FDIC national average rises, the spread compresses. If the Fed cuts and savings APYs follow, the Treasury curve's advantage grows (Treasury yields lock in, savings APYs do not). If you need the money inside 12 months, none of the Treasury comparison applies — a savings account is the only one of these options that is liquid without market risk.
